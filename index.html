Student Profile

Andrey L. Quintela

A fourth-year IT student who spends most days reading code, and who learned in Great Books that reading literature trains the same mind in a different way.

About me

I am a Bachelor of Science in Information Technology student in my final year. Most of my time goes into programming, systems, and my capstone work, where I solve problems one logical step at a time. I believe that reading is the bridge to literacy. Before we can write good code, good reports, or good arguments, we first have to learn how to read carefully and understand what others are saying.

Reading and literature

My reading life used to be mostly practical: documentation, tutorials, and technical articles. I enjoyed stories, but I rarely chose long classics on my own. What draws me to a story is a character who changes, someone who starts out flawed and has to earn their growth. That is why the old epics surprised me. Behind the unfamiliar names and long verses were people dealing with pride, loyalty, grief, and ambition, the same things people deal with today.

What I expected, and what I found

When I enrolled in Great Books, I expected a lot of difficult reading that had little to do with my course. Instead, I found works from Greece, India, Japan, France, and Italy that taught me how to look at one text from many angles, the same way I would test a system from many cases. I expected to memorize plots. I ended up learning how to interpret them, question them, and connect them to my own life.

Analysis 1 of 7 · Comparative study, part 1

Ἰλιάς

The Iliad

by Homer · read alongside The Ramayana

Lens: Comparative (Cultural)Archaic Greecec. 8th century BCEGreek heroic epic · 24 books

Overview

The Iliad covers a few weeks in the tenth year of the Trojan War. Agamemnon, the Greek king, dishonors Achilles by taking his prize, the captive Briseis. Achilles withdraws from battle in wounded pride, and the Greeks suffer heavy losses. His companion Patroclus is killed by the Trojan prince Hector, and Achilles returns to take a destructive revenge. The epic ends with Priam, King of Troy, ransoming his son’s body, and with mourning and funerals.

Main characters: Achilles, the flawed Greek hero; Hector, the noble Trojan prince; Agamemnon; Patroclus; and Priam.

The lens and why I chose it

I analyzed The Iliad together with Valmiki’s Ramayana using a comparative, cultural lens. This approach reads two works from different civilizations side by side to see how each culture answers the same basic human questions. I chose these two epics because comparing them shows how the ancient West and the ancient East understood heroism: Greek individual honor against Indian cosmic duty (dharma).

Analysis

The Iliad is built on the values of the Greek heroic world. Kleos is the immortal glory a warrior earns in battle. Timē is personal honor and public recognition. Aretē is individual excellence. When Agamemnon takes Briseis, he attacks Achilles’ timē, and in this culture that is an attack on his whole worth as a man. That is why one insult can set off so much destruction.

Achilles is a deeply flawed hero, driven by pride and rage. He also faces a painful choice between personal desire and public duty. He knows he can live a long, quiet life at home or die young and win eternal glory. Above everything stands moira, a tragic and indifferent fate. The gods are capricious, taking sides and fighting beside mortals, and fate crushes warriors whether they are virtuous or not. In this world, honor is the only legacy that lasts.

Set beside the Ramayana, the similarities are striking. Both wars begin with the taking of a royal woman, Helen in Greece and Sita in India. In both, divine beings actively shape human battles. The differences are just as revealing. Achilles is flawed while Rama is presented as ideal, and The Iliad is ruled by fate while the Ramayana is ruled by dharma and karma. The tone differs too. The Iliad ends in grief and funerals, with the destruction of Troy still ahead.

True heroism is not defined by physical dominance, but by the moral framework in how we process duty, grief, and sacrifice.

Connection to today: the danger of ego in leadership

Achilles’ refusal to fight over bruised pride cost countless lives. Today, unchecked hubris and personal feuds among corporate and political leaders still harm the communities that depend on them.

Analysis 2 of 7

Ὀδύσσεια

The Odyssey

by Homer

Lens: ArchetypalAncient Greecec. 8th century BCEEpic · 24 books · dactylic hexameter

Overview

After the fall of Troy, Odysseus, king of Ithaca, spends ten years trying to sail home. He blinds the Cyclops Polyphemus, angering the sea god Poseidon. He faces the witch Circe, travels to the Underworld, resists the Sirens, and is held for seven years on the nymph Calypso’s island. Meanwhile, in Ithaca, his wife Penelope holds off more than a hundred suitors, and his son Telemachus goes searching for news of him. Odysseus finally returns disguised as a beggar, defeats the suitors with Telemachus’ help, and is reunited with Penelope after she tests him with the secret of their bed.

The lens

Archetypal criticism, drawn from Carl Jung and Joseph Campbell, looks for universal patterns, characters, and symbols that appear in stories across cultures. Campbell called the most common pattern the “hero’s journey”: departure, trials, a descent into darkness, and a transformed return.

Analysis

The Odyssey follows the hero’s journey almost step by step. Odysseus leaves the known world, faces a series of trials, and makes a literal descent into the Underworld, where he meets the prophet Tiresias and the ghost of his mother. In Jungian terms, this descent is a meeting with death and the past, and the hero cannot return home until he has faced it.

Familiar archetypes fill the story. Athena, often disguised as the old man Mentor, is the wise guide. Circe and Calypso are temptresses who offer pleasure or even immortality in exchange for forgetting home. The Cyclops is a threshold guardian, a monster blocking the way forward. Penelope is the archetype of the faithful spouse, but she is also a trickster in her own right. Her loom, which she weaves by day and secretly unravels by night, shows a mind as clever as her husband’s.

The most important archetypal change is inside Odysseus himself. Early on, after escaping the Cyclops, he cannot resist shouting his real name to boast. That moment of pride brings Poseidon’s curse and costs him his crew. By the end, the same man enters his own palace as a beggar, quietly accepting insults and kicks until the right moment. The hero who once needed the world to know his name has learned to be nobody. His return home is also a return to humility.

The journey home is not only across the sea. It is the slow journey from arrogance to self-control, and the hero only arrives once he has changed.

Connection to today

Every person who leaves home for school or work and struggles to come back as a better version of themselves is living a small odyssey. The story reminds us that pride makes the trip longer.

Analysis 3 of 7 · Comparative study, part 2

रामायण

The Ramayana

attributed to Sage Valmiki · read alongside The Iliad

Lens: Comparative (Cultural)Ancient Indiac. 5th–4th century BCESanskrit epic · 7 kandas

Overview

Prince Rama, heir to the kingdom of Ayodhya, is unjustly sent into exile in the forest for fourteen years. His devoted wife Sita and his loyal brother Lakshmana go with him. The demon king Ravana abducts Sita and carries her to Lanka. With the help of Hanuman and his allies, Rama wages an epic war, defeats Ravana, and rescues Sita. He returns to Ayodhya, where his reign restores moral and cosmic order.

Main characters: Prince Rama, the ideal hero and avatar; Sita, the devoted wife; Lakshmana, the loyal brother; Hanuman; and Ravana, the demon king.

The lens

This is the second half of my comparative study with Homer’s Iliad. The cultural, comparative lens asks how each epic reflects the worldview of the civilization that produced it, and what we learn by placing them side by side.

Analysis

The Ramayana is governed by dharma: unwavering moral duty and righteousness. Rama is called Maryada Purushottama, the ideal and morally flawless man. When his father is bound by a promise to send him away, Rama accepts exile instead of fighting for the throne he deserves. His choice of duty over royal status is the moral center of the epic.

The epic also celebrates filial piety and loyalty, self-sacrifice for family and society. Lakshmana gives up fourteen years of comfort to serve his brother, and Sita chooses hardship in the forest over life in the palace. The story ends in the triumph of good: Ravana’s chaotic forces are defeated by moral integrity, and order is restored.

Compared with The Iliad, the Ramayana shows a very different worldview. The Greek epic follows a flawed hero in a world of indifferent fate. The Indian epic follows an ideal hero in a world ruled by dharma and karma, where moral actions decide outcomes and cosmic order. Greek culture centers on individual glory and mortal fame, while the Ramayana places personal desire below social harmony, respect for parents, and universal righteousness. The Iliad closes in mourning. The Ramayana closes in triumph with the restoration of Rama’s reign.

The tradition also has many versions across Asia, as A. K. Ramanujan shows in his essay on the “many Ramayanas.” One of them is close to home: the Maranao Maharadia Lawana from Mindanao.

Comparing East and West showed me that Western classical literature often highlights human vulnerability and the tragedy of mortality, while the Eastern epic tradition emphasizes the enduring power of moral integrity and spiritual alignment.

Connection to today: ethics over convenience

Rama’s commitment to dharma despite personal hardship is a model of ethical courage today: choosing civic duty, truth, and environmental responsibility over short-term personal gain.

Analysis 4 of 7

पञ्चतन्त्र

The Panchatantra

attributed to Vishnu Sharma

Lens: SociologicalAncient Indiac. 3rd century BCE–300 CEAnimal fables · frame narrative · 5 books

Overview

The Panchatantra, meaning “five treatises,” is a collection of animal fables inside a frame story. A king worries that his three sons are dull and unteachable. The wise Brahmin Vishnu Sharma promises to teach them the art of wise conduct, or niti, in six months through stories. The five books cover losing friends (a scheming jackal destroys the friendship between a lion king and a bull), winning friends (a crow, a mouse, a tortoise, and a deer help one another), war and peace (the long feud between crows and owls), losing what you have gained (a monkey outwits a crocodile), and acting without thinking (a Brahmin kills a loyal mongoose before learning it saved his child).

The lens

The sociological lens examines how a work reflects the society that produced it, including its power structures, social classes, and relationships between people. It asks whose interests the text serves.

Analysis

Although its characters are animals, the Panchatantra is really about human society and power. The forest is a royal court in disguise. The lion is the king, the bull is a newcomer who rises quickly in the king’s favor, and the jackals are ministers who feel pushed aside. The jackal Damanaka spreads lies until the lion kills his new friend. This is a clear picture of court politics, where advisers compete for the ruler’s attention and the truth becomes a weapon.

The book also shows how the weak survive among the strong. Small animals such as the mouse, the crow, and the rabbit win through intelligence and cooperation, not force. Book 2 is especially meaningful because it shows the powerless forming alliances. Four very different animals protect each other, and together they are safer than any of them alone.

At the same time, it matters who the text was written for. It was a handbook for princes, so its morals are practical rather than idealistic. Deception is often praised when it works, and loyalty is valued mainly when it helps the ruler. The Panchatantra reflects a society where survival and political success mattered as much as virtue.

Behind its simple fables is a sharp lesson about how power works: who gets heard, who gets used, and how those with less power protect themselves.

Connection to today

Office politics, gossip, and people who flatter leaders to get ahead still exist. The fables remind us to check information before acting and to value real friendships built on mutual help.

Analysis 5 of 7

源氏物語

The Tale of Genji

by Murasaki Shikibu

Lens: Moral–PhilosophicalHeian Japanc. 1008 CEClassic court novel · psychological romance · 54 chapters

Introduction and context

The Tale of Genji belongs to the Heian period of classical Japanese literature and is widely recognized as the world’s first psychological novel written by a woman. Its author, Murasaki Shikibu, was a noblewoman, poet, and lady-in-waiting to Empress Shōshi. The Heian imperial court valued refined aesthetics, waka poetry, secret romances, and strict aristocratic etiquette. The novel was written in kana script, which allowed women at court to produce rich literature in their own vernacular.

Summary

The youth of the Shining Prince. Emperor Kiritsubo falls deeply in love with a low-ranking concubine, Lady Kiritsubo, and their son becomes known as Hikaru Genji, the “Shining Prince.” Jealous rivals torment his mother until she dies, leaving Genji with a lifelong longing for a mother’s love. To protect him from succession rivalries, the Emperor makes him a commoner under the Minamoto name, barring him from the throne.

Forbidden love and Murasaki. Trapped in a distant arranged marriage with Lady Aoi, Genji searches for his late mother’s image in other women. He falls in love with Lady Fujitsubo, his father’s consort, and their secret affair produces a son who is raised as the Emperor’s child. Later, Genji finds the young Murasaki, Fujitsubo’s orphaned niece. Drawn by her resemblance to his forbidden love, he takes her into his home and raises her to become his lifelong companion.

Scandal and exile to Suma. Genji’s careless affairs come to a head with Lady Oborozukiyo, a consort promised to the rival Crown Prince. When his enemies expose the scandal, he exiles himself to the desolate shore of Suma, where he faces isolation, storms, and deep spiritual reckoning, pouring his loneliness into painting and poetry.

Return to glory. After a political shift, Genji returns to the capital in triumph. His secret son becomes Emperor Reizei and, after learning his true parentage, raises Genji to unprecedented power. Genji builds the lavish Rokujō mansion with Murasaki at its center.

Karmic betrayal and final days. In his later years, Genji marries the young Third Princess, who commits adultery with his nephew Kashiwagi and bears a son, Kaoru. Genji realizes this is the exact karmic punishment for his own betrayal of his father. Soon after, Murasaki dies, and Genji, crushed by the impermanence of life, burns his old love letters and withdraws from the world. His death is marked only by an empty chapter titled “Vanished into the Clouds.”

The lens

The moral–philosophical lens examines the values and beliefs a work expresses and what it teaches about how to live. Here, it means reading the tale through the Buddhist ideas of karma, attachment, and impermanence that shaped Heian thought.

Analysis

The setting moves between the highly structured Imperial Court in Kyoto and the isolated, rugged coast of Suma. Genji himself is charming, politically gifted, and aesthetically refined, yet emotionally flawed. Around him are the women who define his life: Fujitsubo, the forbidden imperial consort; Murasaki, his true and lifelong love, whom he raised from youth; and Lady Rokujō, an aristocratic lover whose jealousy appears as a vengeful spirit.

The internal conflict is Genji’s endless pursuit of idealized love set against his karmic guilt. The external conflict comes from court politics, rumors, rival factions, and succession disputes.

Two themes carry the moral weight of the novel. Karma and retribution means that unlawful actions bring emotional and spiritual suffering later in life. Genji’s affair with Fujitsubo returns to him decades later in the Third Princess’s betrayal. Mono no aware is a deep, empathetic sadness at the transience of beauty and life. The novel’s symbols reinforce this. Cherry blossoms and the changing seasons stand for passing beauty and the brevity of human joy, while the koto and poetry stand for emotional connection and courtly elegance.

Despite absolute power, charm, and wealth, Genji’s life is defined by a cycle of desire and suffering, which ultimately leads him to seek spiritual peace over worldly attachments.

Interpretation and significance

The novel shows that the pursuit of aesthetic and romantic perfection is ultimately hollow. True wisdom comes from recognizing our flaws and accepting the impermanence of life. Its lessons are the inevitability of change, the consequences of uncontrolled desire, and the value of emotional sensitivity.

Connection to today

The tale explores themes that feel modern: grief, obsession, impostor syndrome, and vulnerability in relationships. It also stands as a landmark of early female authorship, with complex female characters who are still studied today.

Analysis 6 of 7

La Chanson de Roland

The Song of Roland

La Chanson de Roland · Author unknown (attributed to Turoldus)

Lens: HistoricalMedieval Francec. 1100Chanson de geste · Old French epic poem

Introduction and authorship

The Song of Roland is a chanson de geste, an Old French epic poem of the medieval period focused on heroism, loyalty, betrayal, honor, and faith. Its author is unknown. The oldest manuscript names a Turoldus, but the story was shaped over generations by jongleurs, traveling performers who sang the poem aloud before it was written down.

Summary

Act I. Ganelon, jealous of his stepson Roland, betrays Charlemagne’s rearguard to the Saracens. Roland leads the rearguard through the pass at Roncevaux and walks straight into a deadly ambush.

Act II. Badly outnumbered, Roland refuses out of pride to blow his horn, the Oliphant, to call for help. He relents only when defeat is certain, but it is too late, and he dies heroically on the battlefield.

Act III. Charlemagne hears the horn and returns to find Roland dead. He pursues and defeats the Saracens. Ganelon is tried for treason and executed, torn apart by horses.

The lens

Historical criticism studies a work in relation to the real events behind it and the period in which it was written, asking how history was changed in the retelling and why.

Analysis

The poem is loosely based on Charlemagne’s campaign in Spain in 778. On his return, the rearguard of his army was attacked at Roncevaux Pass in the Pyrenees, the fog-covered mountains dividing France from Spain. Historically, the attackers were most likely Basque fighters, and the event was probably a local political conflict, not a religious war. Later storytellers transformed it into a great struggle between Christians and Muslims, matching the crusading spirit of their own time. This shows how a society can reshape history to express its ideals.

The main characters reflect those ideals. Roland is the brave but proud knight, Oliver is his wise companion, Charlemagne is the emperor, and Ganelon is the traitor. The conflicts work on several levels: Roland against the Saracens in battle, Roland against Ganelon as loyalty against vengeance inside the Frankish ranks, Christianity against Islam as a holy war, and Roland against himself as pride against prudence.

That last conflict is the tragic core of the poem. Roland embodies the chivalric ideal of bravery, loyalty to Charlemagne, and devotion to faith and comrades. Yet his démesure, or excessive pride, blinds him. By refusing to blow the Oliphant until it is too late, he condemns 20,000 Frankish soldiers to death. The poem leaves us asking whether Roland is a hero or a man destroyed by his own glory.

The poem’s form supports this. It is written in laisses, unequal stanzas that build dramatic rhythm. It uses repetition with variation, retelling scenes with shifting emotion, and epic formulae such as the famous contrast between brave Roland and wise Oliver. Hyperbole exaggerates heroic feats, and foreshadowing hints early at Roland’s fatal pride. Its symbols carry the themes: the Oliphant stands for fatal hubris, Durendal, Roland’s unbreakable sword, stands for the holy warrior, and Roncevaux Pass becomes a place of sacrifice and martyrdom.

Bravery without wisdom can become dangerous. Roland’s pride cost thousands of lives, yet his story endures because it captures the eternal struggle between honor and humility.

Values and connection to today

The poem reflects medieval values: feudal loyalty that binds lord and vassal unto death, Christian duty that turns sacrifice into a sacred purpose, and brotherhood in arms that forms bonds stronger than blood. It also warns that hubris carries a devastating cost. Today, leaders still face the choice between pride and prudence, loyalty is still tested in politics and war, and we still debate when courage becomes recklessness. The poem remains worth reading because it speaks to loyalty, sacrifice, and what we choose to die for.

Analysis 7 of 7

Divina Commedia

The Divine Comedy

by Dante Alighieri

Lens: FormalistMedieval Italyc. 1308–1321Epic poem · 3 canticles · 100 cantos · terza rima

Overview

Lost in a dark wood in the middle of his life, Dante the pilgrim is rescued by the spirit of the Roman poet Virgil. Virgil guides him down through the nine circles of Hell (Inferno), where sinners suffer punishments that match their sins, and up the mountain of Purgatory (Purgatorio), where souls are cleansed. At the top, Beatrice, the woman Dante loved, leads him through the heavenly spheres of Paradise (Paradiso) until he has a final vision of God.

The lens

Formalist criticism focuses on the text itself, including its structure, form, language, and patterns, and on how these elements create meaning without depending on the author’s life or historical context.

Analysis

Few works are as carefully built as the Divine Comedy. Its structure is based on the number three, which represents the Christian Trinity. There are three canticles, each with 33 cantos. With one introductory canto, the total is exactly 100, a number that symbolized perfection. Hell has nine circles, Purgatory has nine levels including its terraces, and Paradise has nine spheres. The whole universe of the poem is built as an ordered design.

Dante also invented a rhyme scheme for the poem, terza rima. Its stanzas of three lines rhyme ABA, BCB, CDC, and so on. The middle line of each stanza sets the rhyme for the next, so each stanza is linked to what follows. This form creates a constant forward movement that mirrors the pilgrim’s journey. Every canticle also ends on the same word, stelle, meaning “stars,” so each part of the journey closes by looking upward.

The principle of contrapasso, punishment that fits the sin, is another formal pattern. Fortune-tellers who tried to see the future walk with their heads twisted backward. The lustful, who were carried away by passion, are blown forever by a storm. Even Dante’s choice to write in Italian instead of Latin is part of the form. It made the poem readable to ordinary people.

In the Divine Comedy, the form is the meaning: a perfectly ordered poem expresses Dante’s belief in a perfectly ordered universe.

Connection to today

As an IT student, I see the poem almost like a well-designed system, with clear architecture, consistent rules, and every part serving the whole. It shows that structure is not just decoration. It shapes how we understand content.

My Favorite Literary Masterpiece

The Odyssey

by Homer · Ancient Greece · c. 8th century BCE

A great man’s hardest journey is not across the sea. It is the journey back to humility.

Brief overview

The Odyssey tells the story of Odysseus, king of Ithaca, and his ten-year struggle to return home after the Trojan War. He faces monsters, gods, temptation, and the loss of every one of his men. At home, his wife Penelope remains loyal and holds off more than a hundred suitors who want her hand and his kingdom. When Odysseus finally returns, disguised as a beggar, he must win back his home with patience instead of pride.

Why it is my favorite

I chose The Odyssey because it is a story about a flawed hero who changes. Odysseus is brilliant, but at the start of his journey he is also arrogant. After blinding the Cyclops and escaping, he could have sailed away safely. Instead, he shouts his name across the water so the Cyclops will know who defeated him. That one moment of pride brings the curse of Poseidon and adds years of suffering to his journey.

What I love is that the story does not end with the same man. By the time he reaches Ithaca, the proud king who boasted to the Cyclops has learned to stay silent. He enters his own home dressed as a beggar and accepts insults from the suitors without revealing himself. His journey home is really a journey back to humility, and he can only reclaim his place once he has learned it.

Character

Odysseus: from pride to humility

His cleverness saves him again and again, but his arrogance nearly destroys him. He shows that talent without humility can become a weakness, and that real strength includes self-control.

Character

Penelope: loyalty with intelligence

For twenty years she stays faithful without knowing if her husband is alive. Her loyalty is not passive. She tricks the suitors with her weaving and tests Odysseus with the secret of their bed. She is his equal.

Themes and lessons that shaped my appreciation

Pride and humility. The epic shows that pride delays our progress and humility brings us home. Loyalty. Penelope, Telemachus, and the faithful swineherd Eumaeus show that loyalty is tested most when someone is absent. Home and identity. Odysseus refuses immortality with Calypso because home, family, and who he is matter more than an easy life. Perseverance. He loses everything, yet he keeps going.

Personal connections and realizations

As a fourth-year IT student, I recognize a little of Odysseus in myself. When I first started getting good at programming, I sometimes trusted my own skills too much and ignored advice, and it cost me time when things broke. Like Odysseus shouting at the Cyclops, I had to learn that confidence is useful, but showing off and refusing help only make the journey longer. Asking questions and listening to others is not weakness.

Penelope also made me think about loyalty in my own life. Her faithfulness reminded me of the people who stay with us during long, uncertain seasons, including family who support our studies and friends who stay through difficult semesters. The Odyssey taught me that the people who wait for us are part of the journey too, and that coming home means returning as someone better than when we left.

Summary of Learning

What Great Books taught me

The course took me through nearly three thousand years of literature and gave me tools to read any text more deeply.

Masterpieces across periods and cultures

I studied works from ancient Greece (The Iliad and The Odyssey), ancient India (The Ramayana and The Panchatantra), Heian Japan (The Tale of Genji), medieval France (The Song of Roland), and medieval Italy (The Divine Comedy). I learned that each work grew out of a specific culture, with ideas such as Greek honor, Indian dharma, Japanese mono no aware (the gentle sadness of passing things), feudal loyalty, and Christian salvation. I also saw how they share the same human concerns.

Literary lenses

I learned that a single text can be read in many ways depending on the lens. The same story can be about the mind, about society, about history, or about form. Applying a lens taught me to support an interpretation with evidence from the text rather than relying on personal opinion alone.

Literature as a mirror of humanity

These works showed me what people believed, feared, and valued long ago. I learned about warrior codes, court life, religious faith, and the limits placed on women. Literature preserves the experiences of people who are long gone, and it shows how much of human nature has stayed the same.

Critical thinking and analysis

Writing seven analyses trained me to read closely, ask questions, compare works, and build an argument step by step. I learned to notice details such as a repeated word, a structural pattern, or a character’s silence, and to explain why they matter. These skills apply directly to research, technical writing, and problem solving in my own field.

Personal Reflection

Looking back on the voyage

1How my understanding and appreciation of literature changed

Before this course, I thought of classic literature as something old and distant, written for scholars rather than for students like me. I read mainly for information, and I judged a text by how quickly I could get something useful out of it. Great Books changed that. I learned that these stories last because they are about people. Achilles’ anger, Odysseus’ pride, Rama’s difficult duty, and Ukifune’s loneliness are still recognizable today. I now see literature as a conversation across time, and I appreciate that a single story can hold many meanings.

2Challenges I encountered and how I overcame them

The biggest challenge was the reading itself. The works are long and full of unfamiliar names, places, gods, and customs. The Divine Comedy and The Tale of Genji were especially hard to follow at first. To overcome this, I read summaries and background notes before the actual text so I knew what to expect, and I kept a simple list of characters and their relationships, almost like documentation for a program. I also learned to read in smaller sections and to ask classmates and our instructor when I was confused. Writing the analyses was another challenge, because I was used to finding one correct answer. I had to learn that interpretation needs evidence, not just a right or wrong answer.

3The lens that influenced me most

The archetypal lens influenced me the most. Once I learned about the hero’s journey and recurring character types, I began to see the same patterns everywhere: in the epics, in films, in games, and even in people’s real lives. It helped me connect works from very different cultures, since Odysseus, Rama, and Dante all leave home, face trials, and return changed. As an IT student, I also liked that the lens works like pattern recognition. It finds the shared structure underneath many different stories.

4Realizations about life, society, culture, and human nature

I realized that human nature has changed very little in thousands of years. People still struggle with pride, jealousy, loyalty, and grief. I also realized that stories are shaped by the societies that tell them. The Song of Roland showed me how history can be rewritten for a cause, and The Tale of Genji showed me how endless desire leads to suffering, and how a woman writer captured that truth a thousand years ago. Most of all, I realized that humility and empathy matter. The Iliad ends when Achilles finally sees his enemy as a fellow human being, and The Odyssey ends only after its hero learns to be humble.

5Applying these lessons in my life and future career

In my personal life, I want to remember the lesson of Odysseus: confidence is good, but pride makes the journey longer, so I should listen to others and ask for help. In my academic life, the close reading and analysis I practiced will help me with research, documentation, and my capstone project. In my future career in IT, I will work in teams, communicate with clients, and solve problems that affect real people. The empathy I learned from these works will help me understand users. The critical thinking will help me question information instead of simply accepting it. Like the pilgrim in Dante’s poem, I know I will sometimes find myself in a dark wood, but this course taught me that every journey can lead to growth.
